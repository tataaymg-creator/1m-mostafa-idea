# LOOK · بُصّ — Story

Working title. Alternates: *Baba, Look* · *The Quiet Game* · *Table for Three*.

## Logline

Four months after the crash that killed his wife and daughter, a man keeps them alive at home as two mannequins from his wife's sewing corner, until the memory he has been refusing returns one fragment at a time, and he finally looks at the last thing his daughter wanted to show him.

## What the film is about

Denial is a refusal to look. So the whole film is built on one word.

The last thing his daughter said to him was **"Baba, look."** She was holding up a drawing. He turned to see it, and that second is the one he cannot forgive or remember. The mannequins have no eyes. The curtains are closed. A sealed hospital bag sits by the door, unopened, with the drawing inside.

The film ends when he looks: at the memory, at the drawing, at the plastic, and at the person knocking on his door.

## The honest problem with this premise, and how we beat it

"Grieving man lives with mannequins, twist: they're dead" is a familiar shape. A jury will guess it within thirty seconds. Three decisions turn that into an advantage:

1. **We give the twist away at 0:30.** The mannequins are shown plainly, with no music sting. The audience stops asking *"what's going on?"* and starts asking *"what happened, and what is he not letting himself remember?"* That question carries six minutes. A twist carries ten seconds.
2. **The real reveal is small and human.** It is the one second he took his eyes off the road, because his daughter asked him to look at her drawing. He was a good father in that second. That is what makes it unbearable.
3. **The ending is an action, not a realisation.** Remembering is not the end. He opens the bag, he opens the curtains, he opens the door. "They're gone, Mona" is the first true sentence he says in the film.

## Rules of the film

These keep it disciplined, and most of them make it easier to generate.

| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| The mannequins never move on their own. He moves them. | It is the truth of the film, and static figures are the most reliable thing AI video can do. |
| We never hear them answer. He leaves pauses the length of a reply. | The silence does the acting. No lip-sync needed. |
| Her voice enters the present exactly once: "Baba, look." | One break of the rule, at 3:30, is the turn of the film. |
| Present = 4:3 frame, locked camera, closed curtains, cool and dusty. | He is boxed in. |
| Memory = full-width frame, handheld, golden hour, warm. | The past is more alive than the present. |
| The frame widens to full width when he opens the curtains. | The form pays off the story. It costs nothing: it is a crop in the edit. |
| Yellow is the only strong colour in the present: her dress, her cup, her sandal, her crayon, the sun in her drawing. | The eye always finds her. |
| He never eats. Three full plates, every meal. | Shows how long this has gone on without a caption. |
| No score until he breaks. Before that, only the clock, the radio and the room. | When music arrives it means something. |
| The crash is never shown. White, silence, a crayon rolling on asphalt. | Stronger than any impact, safe for any content rule, and avoids the physics AI gets wrong. |
| No readable text on screen. | AI text garbles, Arabic especially. |
| Each flashback is triggered by something physical: a click, a hair, a song, a light. | Trauma memory is sensory, not narrated. |

## Characters

- **Adam, 38.** A quiet man, an accountant type, gentle hands. A healed scar at his right temple that he touches when the flashes come. He is not mad and the film never treats him as a horror figure. He is a husband and father doing the only thing that lets him get through the day.
- **Layla, 34.** His wife. A dressmaker who worked from a corner of their living room. Warm, teasing, sings badly and knows it. We meet her only in memory. Her colour is burgundy.
- **Nour, 6.** Their daughter. Stubborn, bright, always drawing. Her name means "light". Her colour is yellow. We meet her only in memory, and hear her once in the present.
- **Mona, 45.** Layla's older sister. A voice through a door for most of the film; a face only in the last thirty seconds. Clearly related to Layla, clearly not her (older, grey in her hair, glasses).
- **The two mannequins.** Layla's display mannequins from the sewing corner: one adult, one child-size. Matte, smooth, abstract faces with no painted eyes. Adam has dressed them in Layla's cardigan and the dress she was still making for Nour.

## The story, beat by beat

**Cold open (0:00).** Black. We hear a car, a crayon on paper, a child humming, a woman laughing, a seatbelt click. We do not know yet that this is the last minute of their lives.

**Morning (0:06).** A stove clicks on. A man sets a table for three, cuts toast into triangles, says good morning to two figures by the window. He tells his daughter to sit up straight and waits for her answer. He bends to kiss his wife's forehead, and we see plastic. He sits between two mannequins and talks through breakfast. He does not eat. The bin is full of other mornings. By the front door, under four months of unopened mail, sits a sealed hospital bag.

He buckles a yellow sandal onto a rigid foot. The click throws him somewhere else for two seconds: a seatbelt, a child's swinging legs. He shakes it off.

**Hair (1:00).** He carries his daughter to the sofa and tries to braid her hair. The wig slips. A flash: wind in a real girl's hair, a hand with a wedding ring tucking it back. He apologises to the mannequin.

**The sewing corner (1:25).** One shot explains everything: Layla's machine still threaded with yellow, the dress's missing sleeve under the needle, two empty stands.

**The song and the dance (1:30).** The radio finds an old song. A flash: the same song in the car, Layla singing along, laughing. He asks his wife to dance, and for a moment, cheek against the wig, he is at peace. Then her arm comes off in his hand. He kneels and puts it back, asking if he hurt her.

**The knock (2:18).** Someone at the door. Mona, Layla's sister, through the wood: *"It's been four months. I lost them too."* He does not open. He tells his daughter they are playing the quiet game. Mona leaves a dish of food and goes. For one second his face shows that part of him knows.

**Dinner (2:46).** The same table, at night. He promises them the sea tomorrow: *"This time we'll get there."* Headlights from the street slide across the two blank faces, and the memory hits harder: a windshield, lights, Layla's smile dropping. He ends the evening.

**Bedtime (3:09).** He tucks the child mannequin in, kisses her, switches off the lamp. In the doorway, in the dark, a child's voice says: *"Baba... look."* He does not turn around. *"Not now, habibti."* The voice comes again, brighter, and the road rushes in.

**The road (3:39).** The whole memory, in order, for the first time. Golden hour, the sea on the horizon, the song, Layla's hand on his. Nour in the back, drawing for an hour, refusing to show anyone. Then: *"Baba, look!"* He says one minute, he is driving. She insists. Layla laughs and tells him to look or she will never stop. He turns his head, and what he sees makes him happier than we have ever seen him. We do not see the drawing; the sun burns it out. Layla says his name. Headlights. White.

Silence. A yellow crayon rolls across asphalt. His open eye, cheek on the road, a sheet of paper drifting past.

**The floor (4:37).** The same eye, on his daughter's bedroom floor. He looks up at the bed: a plastic figure under a blanket. Nothing speaks. He cries, for the first time. Dawn comes.

**The bag (4:59).** He walks to the door and opens the bag he has stepped past for four months. Her watch, stopped. Her ring. A crayon. A folded sheet of paper. He unfolds it. *"I'm looking, habibti. I'm looking."* And we finally see it: three figures holding hands by the sea, under an enormous yellow sun.

**Daylight (5:28).** He opens the curtains, and the frame opens with them. In daylight the mannequins are objects. He takes off his wife's cardigan, folds it, breathes it in. He returns both figures to the sewing corner and lays the unfinished sleeve over the small one's shoulder.

**The door (5:51).** A knock, softer. This time he walks the whole length of the hall and opens it. Mona. He says it: *"They're gone, Mona."* She holds him in the doorway. On the table behind them, the drawing leans against a yellow cup.

Black. Title. A child humming five notes.

## Sound and music

- **The 5-note tune** is the film's spine. Nour hums it in the cold open, Adam hums it off-key while cooking, the radio song is built on it, the score plays it when he breaks, and it completes for the first time over the drawing. The title card leaves it unfinished again.
- **The click**: seatbelt = stove igniter = sandal buckle = mannequin arm = hazard relay = night-light motor. One sound, six disguises.
- **The clock** ticks through every present-day scene except Nour's room.
- All music must be original. No existing songs.
